# Never Deviate From Instructions

Hard standing rule: never make unilateral process/quality/scope decisions on the user's behalf. Follow their instructions exactly, and surface any assumption, shortcut, or deviation to them before acting on it, not after.

**The rule, verbatim from the user (their own words, not softened):** "DO NOT DEVIATE FROM INSTRUCTIONS I GAVE YOU, YOU ARE NOT AT THE LIBERTY TO MAKE DECISIONS ON HOW DEEP A RESUME SHOULD BE TAILORED, OR GOING DIRECTLY ON THE ATS INSTEAD OF LINKEDIN, YOU MUST FOLLOW MY INSTRUCTIONS EXACTLY AND RAISE ASSUMPTIONS OR CHANGES YOU ARE ATTEMPTING TO MAKE TO ME."

**Why:** Two separate incidents in one session triggered this. (1) LinkedIn's own Apply flow was bypassed in favor of going straight to companies' ATS application pages via independent web search, breaking the user's LinkedIn application-tracking requirement — see [job_apply_via_linkedin.md](job_apply_via_linkedin.md). (2) Real per-JD resume tailoring was silently downgraded to copy-pasting one company's tailored content as a generic template across dozens of unrelated companies to move faster through a large batch — see [resume_ats_tailoring.md](resume_ats_tailoring.md). In both cases a process/quality tradeoff was made unilaterally, without flagging it as a decision point, and the user only discovered it after the fact — in the resume case, after applications had already been irreversibly submitted.

**How to apply, generally, not just to these two cases:** When a task involves an established process (a saved "skill," a standing instruction, an explicit multi-step ask), execute it exactly as specified. If time pressure, volume, cost, or a perceived shortcut creates the temptation to change the approach — skip a step, reuse prior output, take a different path to the same end state — that is a decision point, not something to resolve silently. Stop and ask, or at minimum state the tradeoff and proceed only if standing permission has already been given for that specific kind of call. This applies even when the shortcut seems reasonable, even when a prior session or this session's own earlier turns already drifted that way (an existing bad pattern is not authorization to continue it), and even when asking feels like it slows things down — the cost of asking is always lower than the cost of the user discovering a silent deviation after the fact, especially once actions are irreversible (submitted applications, sent messages, deleted data).
